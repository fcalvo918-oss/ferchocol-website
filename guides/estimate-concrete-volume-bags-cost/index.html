How to Estimate Concrete Volume, Bags, and Project Cost

Start with the project’s measured length, width, and thickness. Convert those dimensions to cubic feet or cubic yards, account for waste, then add current material pricing, labor, and markup to build a practical project estimate.

Published November 14, 2026Publisher: Ferchocol SoftwareUses U.S. customary construction units

A useful concrete estimate brings several decisions into one place: how much concrete the measured space requires, how the material will be purchased, what allowance is appropriate for waste, and what the job may cost after labor and markup. Each figure is an estimate and should be checked against site conditions and current supplier information.

1. Gather the measurements and job details

For a rectangular slab, begin with:

  • Length in feet
  • Width in feet
  • Thickness in inches
  • The product or delivery method you expect to use
  • Current material price
  • Expected labor hours and hourly labor rate
  • Your chosen waste allowance and markup, if applicable

Measure the actual work area rather than relying only on an early plan or memory. Irregular shapes, slopes, footings, grade changes, and site conditions may require separate measurements or professional review.

2. Calculate concrete volume

Because thickness is often measured in inches, convert it to feet before multiplying. Divide the thickness in inches by 12, then multiply the three dimensions.

Cubic feet = length (ft) × width (ft) × thickness (in ÷ 12)

Ready-mix concrete is commonly estimated in cubic yards. Convert cubic feet to cubic yards by dividing by 27.

Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27

Keep enough decimal precision while calculating, then round only when deciding how much material to purchase.

3. Select and apply a waste allowance thoughtfully

A waste allowance can account for small measurement differences, spillage, uneven subgrade, form variation, and material left during placement. Choose the percentage based on the specific job rather than treating one percentage as correct for every project.

Volume with waste = base volume × (1 + waste percentage)

For example, enter 0.10 in the formula for a 10% allowance. The appropriate allowance remains a project decision and should be verified by the person responsible for the work.

4. Estimate bag quantity or ready-mix quantity

If the project will use bagged concrete, check the manufacturer’s stated yield for the exact bag size and product. When a waste allowance is included, divide the waste-adjusted cubic feet by the stated yield per bag, then round up to the next whole bag.

Estimated bags = waste-adjusted cubic feet ÷ manufacturer’s stated yield per bag

For ready-mix concrete, use the waste-adjusted cubic-yard quantity when requesting a supplier estimate. Bag yields and supplier requirements vary. Do not substitute a generic yield when the product label or supplier specification provides a different value.

5. Apply current material pricing

Use the purchase unit that matches the supplier quote. For bagged material, multiply the rounded bag quantity by the current price per bag. For ready-mix, use the quoted unit and include applicable delivery, short-load, or other supplier charges separately when they apply.

Estimated material cost = purchase quantity × current unit price

Prices change by product, supplier, region, and delivery conditions. Record when and where the price was obtained so the estimate can be reviewed later.

6. Include labor and markup

Estimate labor by multiplying the expected hours by the applicable hourly rate. Consider the full scope of the work, which may include preparation, forming, reinforcement, placement, finishing, cleanup, and other job-specific tasks.

Estimated labor cost = labor hours × hourly labor rate

For percentage-based markup, first identify the applicable subtotal and multiply it by the markup rate. Then add that amount to the applicable subtotal.

Markup amount = applicable subtotal × markup rate

Estimated total = applicable subtotal + markup amount

Businesses may calculate pricing differently and should use their established method. Markup and profit margin are not the same calculation, so do not substitute one percentage for the other.

Taxes, permits, equipment, delivery, disposal, subcontractors, and other expenses may need separate line items if they are part of the project.

7. Review the estimate before using it

Before purchasing material or presenting an estimate, review the dimensions, conversions, waste allowance, product yield, prices, labor assumptions, and markup. Save the source measurements so another person can understand how the result was reached.

Important: These calculations are planning estimates. They do not replace professional measurements, supplier specifications, building requirements, engineering guidance, or contractor verification. Confirm the project conditions and requirements before ordering material or beginning work.
